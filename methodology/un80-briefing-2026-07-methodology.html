The source, and its status

The readout is built from one source: the official UN transcript of the meeting, obtained from the UN's transcripts portal.

MeetingInformal meeting of the plenary to hear a briefing on the UN80 Initiative — General Assembly, 80th session
Date and length29 July 2026, 2 hours 52 minutes 44 seconds
Transcripttranscripts.un.org/en/asset/k1u/k1ujz9s3tb
Videowebtv.un.org/en/asset/k1u/k1ujz9s3tb
Speakers3 Secretariat briefers, 34 delegations (4 speaking for groups), 81 statements

The transcript is automatic speech recognition, not an official record. The UN transcripts portal produces machine transcription with speaker attribution. It is labelled a preview tool by the UN and is not a verbatim record. For an informal plenary like this one there is no verbatim record (PV) to check it against.

This matters in two ways, both handled below: individual words can be misheard by the machine (so every quote that carries weight was re-checked against the meeting audio), and interpreted speech reaches the transcript through an interpreter's word choices rather than the speaker's.

Known transcription artefacts in this meeting, left uncorrected in the underlying transcript but not carried into the readout: the initiative's name is repeatedly rendered “UNAT”, “UNAD” or “UNHCR” instead of “UN80”; Jens Wandel is introduced once as “James Wondal”; Elizabeth Spehar appears as “Speja”, “Sbar” and “Bihar”; the Peacebuilding and Peace Support Office appears as “PBPSO”, “PPPSO” and “PBBSO”. Names and acronyms in the readout are normalised to their correct forms.

The pipeline

1 CollectFetch the speaker-attributed transcript (JSON + text) from the UN portal.
2 ConvertProduce attributed markdown; spot-check speaker attribution against the video.
3 AnalyseCode consensus, fault lines and per-delegation positions — separately per work package.
4 ScoreApply the evidence rubric to each delegation on each package's axis.
5 VerifyCheck every position-critical quote against the meeting audio.
6 RenderBuild the single-file page; verify plot geometry in a browser.

The rule that governs all of it: no fabrication. Every position stated in the readout is verbatim or close paraphrase from the transcript. Where a delegation did not address a question, it is recorded as silent — never inferred from its bloc, its usual policy, or what it said in other meetings. Five delegations are silent on package 18, five on package 21 and five on package 22; they are excluded from those plots rather than given a default position.

Why three separate analyses of one meeting

The meeting covered three reform packages on a single speakers' list. Each delegation had one three-minute turn and most used it to address all three. The readout could have treated this as one debate. It does not, for two reasons.

  • The packages will diverge. The Secretariat committed on the record to separate consultation tracks for packages 18 and 21, and a separate September deliverable for 22. Future meetings will be package-specific, so each analysis is built to anchor its own sequence over time.
  • The three axes measure different things. A delegation pushing hard on funding reform is not thereby pushing hard on a new results mechanism. Scoring them together would average away the distinction.

Because the packages were debated together, the readout also carries a cross-package comparison the separate analyses cannot produce — which delegations change posture between packages. Twenty of thirty-four do.

Scores are not comparable across packages as numbers. Each package's scale is instantiated separately from its own debate, and package 22's top end is compressed because its office already existed, leaving delegations less to ask for. Compare bands (low / medium / high) and direction of movement, never point differences between one package and another.

How positions were scored

The plots place each delegation by a score, so the horizontal position has to mean something defensible. It is produced by a fixed rubric applied to each delegation's own words.

The three axes

Each package has one scored axis, always “how hard did this delegation push toward one named pole of the package's central disagreement”. The pole is chosen before any delegation is scored.

PackageHigher score means
18 — Fundinga harder push for structural change to the funding architecture (rather than better transparency with donor discretion preserved)
21 — Resultsa harder push for a genuinely new system-wide mechanism (rather than absorbing the aim into existing reporting and governance)
22 — Peacea harder push for consolidated, cross-pillar peacebuilding capacity (rather than guarding pillar boundaries and mandate integrity)

Note on package 21's axis. Nearly every delegation used a supportive verb and then attached a condition. Scoring stated support would have put almost the whole room in one band and hidden the politics. The axis therefore measures how much new machinery a delegation is willing to authorise — which is where the conditions do their work.

Order of operations

  • The normal analysis is done first — consensus, fault lines, poles. Scoring decomposes an existing claim; it never substitutes for the analysis.
  • The rubric is instantiated from this transcript, with anchor quotes for each band, before any delegation receives a single point.
  • Each delegation is then scored against those anchors only, reading its full statement, without looking at other delegations' totals or any expected ordering.

The rubric

Seven positive indicators totalling 100 points, plus negative markers subtracted. The indicators are categories of speech behaviour, which is what lets the same rubric work for a funding debate and a peacebuilding debate.

IndicatorMaxWhat earns points
AEndorsement of the proposal20How emphatically the delegation embraces the package. “We see merit in all proposals” scores above “takes note of”.
BDefence against dilution10Spending capital defending the proposal against objections, non-payment or the status quo — distinct from merely endorsing it.
CMaximal ask15Making the strongest concrete demand present anywhere in that debate. The ceiling is found empirically from the most forward-leaning speaker.
DSpecificity15Named recommendations, named instruments, numbers, named institutions — against generic support.
EObligation framing15A ladder: must / obligation > calls on > welcomes / encourages > takes note > silent.
FMaterial commitment10Money, staffing or concrete instruments offered or demanded.
GVerb register15The dominant verbs across the whole statement: demand / must > call / ensure > welcome > take note.
NNegative markers−Boundary conditions on the proposal, challenges to its premise, or demands that it be halted or absorbed into existing machinery.

One weight was adjusted. On package 21, indicator F has almost no expression — no delegation offered money for a results mechanism. Rather than drop it, F was re-instantiated for that package as engagement with the mechanism's cost and implementation, keeping seven indicators and a total of 100. F scores are therefore not comparable between package 21 and the other two.

Reading the numbers

  • Score is the displayed total out of 100, floored at zero. Net is the pre-floor sum including negatives, and is what orders the dots. Both are shown in the “Scores at a glance” table.
  • The scale is ordinal, not interval. It supports “argued harder than”. A 76 is not twice a 38. Dots are spaced by rank, not by score value.
  • Ties are kept and marked with =. A tie between delegations with very different profiles is a finding, not an error — El Salvador and Russia tie at the bottom of package 21 by entirely different routes.
  • Briefers are not scored. The three Secretariat officials authored the proposals, so their support is definitionally maximal. They appear as dashed, dotted-outline dots pinned to the right.

Verification of the scores

All 89 scored rows across the three packages were machine-checked for indicator sums, the floor rule, band assignment and per-indicator caps: 31/31, 29/29 and 29/29 rows clean. All 673 evidence quotes in the plots' evidence panels were checked to appear exactly once, verbatim, in the transcript. The cross-package comparison table was regenerated from the scoring sheets and matched on all 34 rows.

How to read the plots

  • Left to right is the score, banded into three fixed thirds — low (0–39), medium (40–66), high (67–100). Left is weakest, right is strongest, and this means the same thing on all three plots. Cut points are fixed thirds of the scale rather than quantiles, so a lopsided split is a finding about the debate rather than an artefact of the method.
  • Colour is the grouping, and nothing else. Grouping and intensity are different questions and they frequently disagree, which is the point of separating them.
  • Up and down is whichever dimension is selected in the dropdown. A delegation's column never changes when the vertical dimension changes — the column comes from its score.
  • Click a dot to see every indicator, its points and the verbatim quote that earned them, including indicators recorded as “not stated”. Click a second to compare two delegations side by side.

The groupings

Five groups, coloured consistently across the prose, tables and all three plots so a reader can follow one delegation through the page.

  • Western donors — EU member states, the UK, Switzerland, Japan, Republic of Korea
  • CANZ and Pacific Islands Forum
  • G77 and China members speaking nationally
  • Structural sceptics — Russia, Iran, Cuba, China, who challenged the design of the proposals themselves
  • Secretariat briefers — unscored

Groupings reflect how a delegation positioned itself in this meeting, not formal membership alone. The palette was checked under simulated deuteranopia and protanopia; the closest pair separates at ΔE 10.4. Colour is never the only carrier — dots print country codes and the legend names every group in words.

The “differs from camp” filter

Each Positions by delegation grid carries a filter that isolates the delegations whose score sits furthest from the rest of their grouping on that package. A delegation appears when its score is 20 or more points above or below its camp’s median for that package, and it carries a badge reading Pushed harder than camp or Pushed less than camp. Twelve of the eighty-nine delegation cards qualify: five on package 18, three on package 21, four on package 22.

The filter is computed from the scores already printed on the cards. It adds no new coding and infers nothing: a delegation is flagged only on the arithmetic distance between two numbers shown on the page.

What the badge claims, and what it does not. Each package scores a different axis, so the badge is read against that package’s own pole and nothing else. A delegation above its camp on package 18 pushed harder for structural change to the funding architecture than the rest of its camp did; on package 21 the pole is a genuinely new system-wide results mechanism, and on package 22 it is consolidated cross-pillar peacebuilding capacity. Because the three axes measure different things with different ceilings, the badge never compares one package to another, and a delegation flagged on one package carries no implication for how it argued on the others.

A low score has more than one cause. The axis is signed: a delegation arguing against the scored pole accrues negative markers and lands low, and so does a delegation that engaged the package lightly. Japan on package 18 (27) and Russia on package 18 (28) sit near each other for opposite reasons. The badge marks distance from a camp, not the reason for it. The stance line on each card carries that distinction, which is why the badge is never shown without it.

The brevity marker

A delegation whose statement fell below 70% of the meeting's median length carries a brief chip beside its name. The median was 369 words; the threshold 258. Two delegations qualify: Brazil (248 words) and Ethiopia (242). A short statement has fewer chances to trigger indicators, so the marker is a caveat on the score — but it is a fact about speaker behaviour, not a defect in the data. Ethiopia scored in the high band on package 18 precisely because it spent its whole short turn on one package.

Quote verification against the meeting audio

Because the transcript is machine-produced, every quote that carries analytical weight was checked against the recording rather than trusted.

Method. The English-language audio track was downloaded from the UN Web TV video platform. For each quote, the word-level timestamps in the transcript JSON gave a time window; that window was sliced out with ffmpeg and independently re-transcribed with a local speech model (whisper.cpp, large-v3-turbo, with anti-hallucination settings). The two texts were then compared.

Result: 21 of 21 position-critical quotes confirmed against the audio. These are every quote in the Talking Points section plus every quote anchoring a fault line. One correction was applied.

The correction

The portal transcript rendered the Special Adviser's closing line on package 21 as “because the concept is It’s only generated as a concept” — a machine garble. The audio is unambiguous: “the concept is only generated as a concept”. The readout quotes the corrected wording. The claim it supports is unchanged.

What the near-misses were

Six quotes failed an initial automated pass and all six resolved as method artefacts rather than substantive errors: two were spelling conventions (the speech model writes “programs” and “Recommendation 5” where the portal wrote “programmes” and “recommendation five”), two were windows that cut the phrase at the slice boundary, and two sat 10–30 seconds away from their recorded timestamp and were found with a wider probe.

Scope of this check. The 21 quotes above are audio-verified. The 673 evidence quotes inside the plots' click-through panels are verified to be verbatim-exact against the transcript, but are ASR-sourced rather than audio-confirmed. An error there would move a dot within its band rather than change a stated claim.

Limitations

  • Interpretation. Cuba, Mexico, France, Switzerland, China and Senegal spoke through interpretation. Indicators E and G judge verb strength, which for those delegations reflects the interpreter's word choice as much as the speaker's.
  • Statement length. Speaking time was capped at three minutes (five for groups) and enforced with an automatic microphone cut-off — Angola, Senegal and Pakistan were cut off mid-sentence. Group statements had the longer slot and a structural advantage on the specificity and material-commitment indicators. Flagged rather than corrected, because correcting would mean inferring what a delegation would have said.
  • Package 22's compressed ceiling. The office being scored already existed, so the strongest available ask is smaller than in the other two packages.
  • One meeting. This is a first waypoint for all three packages. It establishes a baseline; it does not show movement. Positions here are a reading of one session, not of a delegation's settled policy.
  • No verbatim record. Informal plenaries produce none, so there is no official text to check the ASR against. The audio is the highest authority available, which is why quotes were checked against it directly.
  • Not an official document. This is an independent analysis. It is not produced by, endorsed by, or affiliated with the United Nations.
