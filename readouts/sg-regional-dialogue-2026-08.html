Executive Summary
What kind of meeting this was. Not a negotiation and not a formal debate. Uruguay, holding the CELAC Pro Tempore Presidency, convened the region's own candidates for the first time in the history of a Secretary-General selection, and asked them questions its foreign ministers wrote. Nothing was being decided in the room, so the useful question is not who won an argument but where the region converged — which ideas have backing, and who a delegation would approach to build on them.

The convergence is close to complete, and it runs through states and candidates alike. Every delegation that spoke endorsed the same proposition: after 35 years, the next Secretary-General should come from Latin America and the Caribbean, and after 80 years it should be a woman. What makes the session worth reading is that the region corrected its own argument in real time. The claim of a turn owed was raised and then set aside in favour of a claim about experience — a shift the moderator opened, Grinspan named, and Espinosa endorsed in the next answer.

Three candidates took the five rounds of questions live. Two did not appear in a form that allowed any questions to reach them, which shapes what this record can and cannot say about them:

Two groupings that political distance would not predict are worth naming, because they are where the regional position is actually load-bearing. Argentina under Milei and Venezuela under Rodríguez arrive at the same rotation claim from opposite directions — Argentina framing it as a duty to supply the best-qualified individual, Venezuela as the region's collective political voice. And on whether the region should consolidate behind one candidate, Uruguay and Venezuela give the same answer against the conventional wisdom: multiple candidacies are normal UN practice, not a regional failure.

The single substantive place where a candidate steps away from the room is Rodrigues-Birkett on energy, saying fossil fuels will remain in use for some time and industry has to be engaged to find the balance. Nobody contradicted her, and nobody else said it.

What Was Asked, and Who Answered

Foreign Minister Mario Lubetkin opened by grounding the event in the CELAC heads-of-state consensus agreed in Colombia, and pre-empted the criticism the format invites — that the region has not consolidated behind one name. He answered it with precedent: six Asia-Pacific candidates preceded Ban Ki-moon's selection in 2006, and the European field was not small in 2016.

Where this sits in the selection. The Secretary-General is appointed by the 193-member General Assembly on the Security Council's recommendation, where any permanent member can block a candidate. Security Council straw polls began on 30 July 2026 — four days before this dialogue — with a recommendation anticipated in September and the appointment later in the year. Guyana told the room that early indications from the Council already point toward a woman from the region. This session is therefore the region's own hearing, held after the decisive phase had already moved behind closed doors in New York.

The moderator put five thematic questions to the three live candidates, 90 seconds each, in an order rotating by notarised draw. A sixth was asked by Alexander Acevedo, a law student at the Universidad de la República, on how to win back a generation's trust.

The five rounds

  1. Legitimacy, reform and leadership. Where would you concentrate the UN's political capital, and what structures or practices would you revise?
  2. Peace and security beyond interstate war. If peace also means institutions that protect people and resist capture by violent actors, what changes?
  3. Youth. How to recover the trust of a generation growing up with authoritarianism, disinformation and polarisation — and what role should young people play?
  4. Human rights, international law and equality. How would the region's experience become a priority of the office?
  5. Financing the transition. How to reconcile climate action, inequality and the development aspirations of the Global South?

Who answered whom

All three live candidates addressed the ministerial questions, in noticeably different registers. No question was left unaddressed by all three.

CandidateHow they handled the floor's questions
Carolyn Rodrigues-Birkett Worked through them one by one by name — Brazil, Guatemala, Panama, Colombia, Paraguay on Haiti, Suriname — the most systematic response to the room in the record, drawing on two years on the Security Council and her chairmanship of the Haiti Sanctions Committee.
María Fernanda Espinosa Answered Brazil, Honduras, Paraguay on Haiti, Suriname and Bolivia by name, and said she had written every question down for the transition plan she is preparing with her team.

Also speaking: Enrique Iglesias, the Uruguayan elder statesman, on the UN as a form of coexistence that survives disagreement; and Carolina Cosse, Vice-President of Uruguay, present in the room.

Areas of Consensus

The agreed ground here is wider than in any readout in this series. Everything below was stated by all three live candidates, or by the great majority of the states that spoke.

  • The post should go to Latin America and the Caribbean. Stated by Uruguay, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Guyana, Antigua and Barbuda, Costa Rica, Venezuela, Guatemala, Panama, Colombia, Honduras, Paraguay, Suriname and Bolivia. The grounds are geographic rotation and the 35 years since Javier Pérez de Cuéllar.
  • It should be a woman. Brazil, Mexico, Colombia, Costa Rica and Guyana say so directly, usually as 80 years without one. Mexico quotes President Sheinbaum: it is time for women.
  • Turn alone is not the argument. The region declines to rest on rotation and argues from experience instead — a nuclear-weapon-free zone, a tradition of peaceful settlement, and a developed regional human-rights system.
  • The three pillars are a package, not a menu. Peace and security, development, and human rights must be worked together. Named by all three live candidates and by Guatemala and Bolivia.
  • Prevention and mediation have to come back to the centre. The shared diagnosis is that people in conflict zones ask where the UN is. Brazil, Guatemala, Colombia and Bolivia all press it; every candidate accepts it.
  • Reform means culture, not only budget. UN80 sets a direction but cutting budgets and staff is not a transformation. Espinosa and Grinspan both target duplication and unhealthy inter-agency competition.
  • The Secretary-General must be perceived as impartial, not merely claim it. Bachelet's letter, Espinosa and Grinspan all make the perception the operative test, and all three tie it to the absence of double standards.
  • Debt and the cost of capital are development blockers. Grinspan, Espinosa and Rodrigues-Birkett all name access to concessional finance; Suriname and Colombia press it from the floor.
  • Young people belong inside the institution, not in consultations about it. All three live candidates reject a consultative role as sufficient.
  • Regional organisations are partners, not audiences. Cooperation with CELAC, CARICOM, Mercosur, the OAS and ECLAC is endorsed by every candidate and requested by Paraguay and Colombia.
Open Questions

Because this was a dialogue rather than a negotiation, the items below are things still to be worked out, not boundaries between camps. Each names who said what; fuller positions are in the reference grid.

1. Does the region want one candidate, or five? — everyone agrees plurality is legitimate; nobody says how a common message is produced without it.
Plurality is normal
  • UruguayAnswers the criticism with precedent and says no consolidating mechanism is required: UN practice asks a region for a diversity of candidacies, not a single one (full quote below).
  • VenezuelaAgrees, and moves the target: the aim is not one candidacy but one political message about the region's claim (full quote below).
Asks for unity without naming an instrument
  • GuatemalaAsks the region to show unity and determination to secure the post, without proposing how that would be organised.

Nobody proposed a consolidation mechanism, and the straw polls that began on 30 July will settle the question before the region does.

2. What actually restores the Secretary-General's mediation role? — all three want it back; the machinery is where they separate.
Build a unit for it
  • GrinspanSays the Secretariat has no good-offices office and would create one, so that mediation, prevention and diplomatic action carry the Secretary-General's own legitimacy. Argues from the Black Sea grain agreement: the UN did not stop the war but restored trade and averted a food crisis, and she negotiated with Russia to do it.
Build an earlier tripwire
  • EspinosaPuts the emphasis one step earlier, on prevention as an operating principle rather than a slogan, and would establish an early-warning and early-action hub at member states' disposal.
Build nothing; reorganise
  • Rodrigues-BirkettDeclines new initiatives on principle and would reorganise the response instead, appointing envoys the conflict parties trust and having the Secretary-General pick up the phone directly (full quote below).
Diagnosis without a proposal
  • GrossiHis video reaches the loss but not a remedy: Pérez de Cuéllar acting concretely on the Iran–Iraq ceasefire, Afghanistan, Namibia's independence and demilitarisation in Angola and Cambodia is what the office no longer does (full quote below). The format gave him no opportunity to be asked what he would do about it.
3. Geographic representation in senior posts got one number and no mechanism — Honduras asked for concrete measures.
Where the answers landed
  • EspinosaSupplies the figure — Latin America and the Caribbean hold under 7% of UN posts — and commits to work on equity and parity, treating representation as a management necessity rather than an ornament.
  • GrinspanGoes at an adjacent lever: entry-level P2 posts have gone unfunded through the liquidity crisis and the examinations that let unrepresented countries bring people in have lapsed for years. Notes 3% of UN staff are under 30.
  • Rodrigues-BirkettAddresses staffing here mainly through resourcing the system to member states' needs; she had raised under- and unrepresented countries directly at the July town hall.

No candidate named a target, a timeline or an appointment procedure. This is the clearest gap between a question asked and an answer given.

4. Haiti has support and no costed plan — Paraguay asked how resolution 2793 gets implemented.
Where the answers landed
  • Rodrigues-BirkettGives the fullest answer, and it is largely a list of unfunded problems: she negotiated the resolution and chaired the Sanctions Committee, and names that more than half of gang members are children, that the economy needs rebuilding, that reforestation bears on disaster resilience — and that raising the funds will be extremely important.
  • EspinosaCommits firmly to implementing the gang-suppression force and pairs it with a broader development and opportunity approach for the country.

Neither named a funding source, which is the part Paraguay's question was pointing at.

5. Financing: a shared diagnosis and an unspecified ask — and the session's one real divergence, on energy.
The shared diagnosis
  • GrinspanBrings the numbers: 3.4 billion people live in countries spending more on debt service than on education or health (full quote below); developing countries pay four to eight times the interest rates; all of Africa receives 2% of renewable-energy foreign investment. Locates the fix in development banks taking more risk to crowd in private capital.
  • EspinosaAsks for deep debt restructuring — regional debt is 45% of GDP — and for vulnerability to be counted alongside per-capita income, with the Multidimensional Vulnerability Index as the instrument.
The one substantive step away from the room
  • Rodrigues-BirkettStates a caveat no one else states: fossil fuels will continue to be used for some time given where the transition stands, and member states, agencies and industry have to engage to find the right balance (full quote below). She frames it against 650 million people without electricity, two billion cooking on polluting fuels, and rising AI data-centre demand.

Nobody contradicted her and nobody echoed her. In a session this convergent, an uncontested outlier is worth flagging rather than smoothing over.

Only one of them said it
  • GrinspanThe only speaker to argue the organisation has become too conservative and must accept the cost of rejection and try again — the UN can fail fifteen times and continue, where a government cannot (full quote below). Also the only one to read the Charter as a bargain in which the 1945 victors accepted restraint on their own power.
  • Rodrigues-BirkettThe only candidate to draw the line between Secretariat reform, which is the Secretary-General's own authority, and structural reform, which is not — and to note that if the structural work is unfinished by January she would be implementing decisions rather than designing them.
  • EspinosaThe only one to quantify the meeting load, at more than 2,500 meetings a year, and the only one to offer the UN's relationship with the African Union as the working model for a UN–CELAC relationship.
  • ArgentinaThe only delegation backing the sole male candidate, and it still states the rotation claim as plainly as anyone in the room (full quote below).
  • VenezuelaThe only delegation to open by thanking the region for earthquake solidarity, and the only one to name unity in diversity as the frame for a plural field.

What Each Would Build

The most checkable thing this dialogue produced. Each live candidate was asked where they would put the office's political capital, and each named something specific enough to be held to in 2027. Listed in the order they first spoke.

CandidateStated intentions
Carolyn Rodrigues-Birkett Continuous Secretariat reform under the Secretary-General's own authority, bringing the Secretariat closer to member states, while supplying objective information for the structural decisions that belong to states — expecting to implement rather than design them if the process runs past January. Increase the use of the peace and security tools without creating new initiatives: reorganise the response, appoint mediators and envoys the conflict parties trust, work with regional organisations and act before conflicts break out. Implement resolution 2793 on Haiti, accompanying the country for years. Advocate the Paris Agreement commitments and climate finance for the most vulnerable, and partner with the international financial institutions on concessional access. Prudent management of resources and engagement with member states in arrears. Strengthen the Youth Office's communication with young people.
Rebeca Grinspan Return peace and security to the centre and create a good-offices office inside the Secretariat, which she says does not exist, so mediation, prevention and diplomatic action carry the Secretary-General's legitimacy directly. Take reform past what UN80 has touched, attacking duplication and unhealthy inter-agency competition. Restore entry-level access for young people by funding P2 posts and restarting the examinations that let unrepresented countries bring people in. Work outward to civil society and regional organisations rather than expecting everything to be resolved inside the UN. Press development banks to take more risk so private capital reaches developing countries, and put debt and the cost of capital at the top of the development agenda.
María Fernanda Espinosa Reinstate prevention as an operating principle and build an early-warning and early-action hub for member states. Move the UN80 reform from budget and staff cuts to institutional culture — accountability, results and impact rather than the volume of reports and meetings. Recover the Secretary-General's mediating role in person, with the independence and perceived impartiality to take real risks. Connect the three pillars as a continuum. Push debt restructuring and vulnerability-weighted criteria alongside per-capita income, using the Multidimensional Vulnerability Index. Work toward equity and parity in staffing, from a base under 7% for the region. Build a UN–CELAC relationship on the model of the UN–African Union one.
Rafael Mariano Grossi video No commitments given. The message is a historical argument that the Secretary-General has lost the concrete conflict-resolution role Pérez de Cuéllar exercised. Argentina's foreign minister made the fuller case on his behalf, on four decades of multilateral service, independence, and crisis management at the IAEA.
Michelle Bachelet letter No machinery named. The letter commits to how the office would be exercised: independence, impartiality, judgement and courage; listening to all without becoming anyone's instrument; building agreements without abandoning Charter principles; and acting with the same firmness against any violation of rights or international law, without double standards. Brazil and Mexico set out the fuller case as co-sponsors.

Reference: Speakers at a Glance
Showing 20 of 20

María Fernanda Espinosa

Antigua and Barbuda · Former President of the UN General Assembly; former Minister of Foreign Affairs and of Defence · in the room

Build a prevention hub

Frames the task as recovering the Charter's original role — prevent conflict, build peace, protect people — and says prevention has been crowded out by emergencies, so she would build an early-warning and early-action hub. On reform: UN80 sets the direction but cutting budgets and staff is not transformation; the target is institutional culture, with accountability and impact replacing the count of reports and the 2,500-plus meetings a year. Wants a Secretary-General with free hands, partial only to the Charter and international law, without double standards. Cites the region as a laboratory of international law — the Tlatelolco Treaty, the Inter-American human rights system. On development, the paradox framing: 40% of biodiversity, 30% of fresh water, half the lithium reserves, and the most unequal region after sub-Saharan Africa, with organised crime costing around 15% of GDP and debt at 45%. Puts the region under 7% of UN posts and commits to parity. Offers the UN–African Union relationship as the CELAC model.

Rebeca Grinspan

Costa Rica · Secretary-General of UNCTAD (on leave for the campaign); former Vice-President of Costa Rica · in the room

Build a mediation unit

Opens by rejecting the turn framing and substituting experience. Three priorities: peace and security first, because the Secretariat has no good-offices office and mediation must carry the Secretary-General's own legitimacy; reform beyond what UN80 has touched, against duplication and unhealthy competition; and cooperation on the future. Argues from the Black Sea grain agreement — the UN did not stop the war but restored trade and averted a food crisis, and she negotiated with Russia to do it. Reads the Charter as a package rather than a menu, and as a 1945 bargain in which the victors accepted restraint on their own power. On youth: 3% of UN staff are under 30 and P2 entry examinations have lapsed, so open the doors and go out to civil society, because the UN is unique but not alone. On finance: 3.4 billion people in countries spending more on debt service than health or education, developing countries paying four to eight times the interest rates, and 2% of renewable-energy investment reaching Africa. Closes that the organisation has become too conservative and must accept rejection and try again. Daughter of Second World War refugees.

Carolyn Rodrigues-Birkett

Guyana · Permanent Representative of Guyana to the United Nations; former Minister · online, live

Reorganise, build nothing new

The most systematic answerer of the ministers, working through their questions one by one by name. States the turn argument plainly and pairs it with competence and the CARICOM endorsement. Draws the line between Secretariat reform, which is the Secretary-General's authority and would be continuous, and structural reform, which belongs to member states — her job there is supplying objective information, and she expects to be implementing rather than designing if the process runs past January. Declines to create new initiatives, preferring reorganisation, trusted mediators and envoys the parties accept, and a Secretary-General who picks up the phone. Describes her leadership as practical, inclusive and pragmatic, but always principled. Cites the region's first international court, the 1948 Pact of Bogotá, and use of the ICJ, CARICOM and the OAS. Two years on the Security Council; led its delegation to Colombia; negotiated resolution 2793 and chaired the Haiti Sanctions Committee. On climate: 650 million without electricity, two billion cooking on polluting fuels, AI data-centre demand — and the session's one dissent, that fossil fuels will be used for some time.

Rafael Mariano Grossi

Argentina · Director General, International Atomic Energy Agency · pre-recorded video message; took no questions

Did not take questions

Appeared only through a short video message after a flight change; the host said he would connect live if he landed in time, and he did not. The message is a single reflection rather than a programme. He recalls Javier Pérez de Cuéllar as the region's only Secretary-General and argues that what he demonstrated has been lost: an office acting concretely on war and peace — the ceasefire after the Iran–Iraq war, Afghanistan, Namibia's emergence as an independent nation, demilitarisation in Angola and Cambodia. No reform proposal and no answer to any ministerial question is on this record, because the format put none to him. Argentina's foreign minister made the substantive case for him in the ministerial segment.

Michelle Bachelet

Chile · Former President of Chile; former UN High Commissioner for Human Rights; former Executive Director of UN Women · letter read aloud; took no questions

Did not take questions

Absent with an acute viral illness that cost her her voice, with medical advice against flying; her letter was read out by the moderator at the opening. It endorses the regional claim and the case for a woman after 80 years, then makes its real argument about how the office is exercised rather than who holds it: the Secretariat requires independence, impartiality, judgement and courage, and whoever leads it will face permanent pressure from governments, powers and interests — so they must listen to everyone but be no one's instrument, build agreements without abandoning Charter principles, and act with the same firmness against any violation of rights or international law, without double standards. Brazil and Mexico co-sponsor her candidacy and made the fuller case in the ministerial segment.

Uruguay

Mario Lubetkin, Minister of Foreign Affairs · host, CELAC Pro Tempore President

CELAC state

Convened the dialogue as an implementation of the CELAC heads-of-state consensus reached in Colombia, and says Uruguay does not treat that consensus as ceremonial. Pre-empts the criticism that the region is divided, citing at least six Asia-Pacific candidates before Ban Ki-moon in 2006 and a substantial European field in 2016: UN practice asks a region for a diversity of candidacies, not a single one. Argues the region brings more than rotation — a consistent defence of international law and peaceful settlement, and the only region at peace and denuclearised. Notes more than 15 foreign ministers following the session.

Argentina

Pablo Quirno, Minister of Foreign Affairs, International Trade and Worship

CELAC state

Nominates Grossi and states the regional rotation claim as plainly as any delegation in the room, despite backing the field's only man. Invokes President Milei on the organisation needing to recover its capacity to act, its credibility and its efficiency, and says administering consensus and preserving bureaucratic inertia is no longer enough. Argues the region's opportunity is also a responsibility: it should field leadership recognised for trajectory, independence and the capacity to mediate. Makes the case on four decades of multilateral service, presence in the most sensitive theatres while channels closed elsewhere, and IAEA technical cooperation for the region in health, agriculture, food security and water.

Brazil

Mauro Vieira, Minister of Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Co-sponsors Bachelet with Mexico. Says the world faces the highest number of armed conflicts since the Second World War alongside a worrying idea that international law is selective, optional or irrelevant — while the great majority of states remain committed to the Charter and reject that force makes right. Argues 35 years after the region's only Secretary-General it is time for a woman from the region. Mexico

Enrique Ochoa, Vice-Minister of Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Co-sponsors Bachelet with Brazil. Treats rotation as an agreement the region has honoured and that lends stability in an uncertain moment, and says the region has a proven record of multilateralism as method and international law as principle, consolidating its territory as a zone of peace. Argues that after 80 years it is time for a woman, quoting President Sheinbaum that today is the time of women. Makes the case for Bachelet on two terms as head of state, her defence background, her medical training, and her UN roles at the Human Rights Office and UN Women.

Guyana

Delivered on behalf of Hugh Todd, Minister of Foreign Affairs and International Cooperation

CELAC state

Nominated Rodrigues-Birkett, in whom it says Guyana and CARICOM have the fullest confidence, describing her as at once principled and practical, a problem solver and consensus builder. Sets out the incoming Secretary-General's inheritance: the highest number of conflict situations since the Second World War, rising geopolitical tension, multilateralism under stress, the SDGs falling behind and the climate crisis unmitigated. Reports that early indications from the Security Council point toward a woman from Latin America and the Caribbean, in keeping with the community's advocacy. Commends all the other candidates.

Antigua and Barbuda

Paul Chet Greene, Minister of Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Nominated Espinosa, and is explicit that the nomination rests neither on her being highly qualified nor on her being Antiguan, but on her representing the best the Americas can offer. Frames the selection as deciding the kind of United Nations wanted for the next decade rather than choosing an individual: restoring confidence, building consensus, delivering results through principled and patient diplomacy. Acknowledges that many delegations are still weighing their position, and asks them to consider who is best placed to build confidence across the whole system, work with the Security Council while keeping the General Assembly's trust, and advance reform without creating unnecessary division.

Costa Rica

Adriana Bolaños Argueta, Vice-Minister for Multilateral Affairs

CELAC state

Presents Grinspan as Costa Rica's candidate, citing vision, experience and diplomatic judgement, and a proven capacity to build bridges, generate dialogue and reach agreements. Summarises her programme as rebuilding trust in the United Nations, strengthening preventive diplomacy, improving the organisation's effectiveness, advancing sustainable development, promoting respect for international law and ensuring tangible results for member states and their peoples. Endorses the concept note's framing that the time has come for the region, and that after 80 years a woman should lead.

Venezuela

Minister of Foreign Affairs and International Trade

CELAC state

Opens by thanking the region for solidarity after two earthquakes. Calls the moment a historic opportunity after 35 years, consistent with rotation, and a chance to strengthen the organisation's credibility. Does not endorse a candidate: the best will be whoever shows firm commitment to the Charter, the courage to defend its purposes and principles, and above all the capacity to build bridges and consensus. Argues for unity in diversity — the aim is not a single candidacy but a common political message about the legitimacy of the region's aspiration, because when the region acts together it strengthens multilateralism as well as its own voice.

Guatemala

Carlos Ramiro Martínez Alvarado, Minister of Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Describes the next Secretary-General as the Charter's custodian across all three pillars, inheriting persistent wars in Ukraine, Sudan and the Middle East, widening economic gaps, the rapid advance of artificial intelligence and intensifying competition between the major powers. Argues the region should show unity and determination to secure the post and help recover public confidence in the institution and the rules-based order, without proposing how that unity would be organised. Asked the candidates what initiatives they would advance to strengthen prevention, peaceful settlement and the authority of international law.

Panama

Javier Eduardo Martínez-Acha Vásquez, Minister of Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Frames the regional claim as equity rather than privilege: a genuinely universal organisation should reflect the diversity of the regions it represents. Wants a leader with international credibility who can sit with the major powers and still generate trust between deeply divided states — a respected interlocutor in Washington and Beijing, Moscow and Brussels, north and south alike — and says moral authority is built rather than proclaimed. Argues the region arrives without hegemonic pretensions, with a tradition of listening before imposing and mediating before confronting. Asked what the candidates would do to make the organisation once again the main negotiating space between those who no longer talk to each other.

Colombia

Mauricio Jaramillo, Vice-Minister for Multilateral Affairs

CELAC state

Backs the region's return to the post after more than 35 years and a first woman in more than 80 years, arguing both strengthen the organisation's legitimacy and representativeness. Put two concrete questions: how the Secretary-General should strengthen good offices, preventive diplomacy and mediation — and specifically what the organisation's role should be for peace and security in Latin America and the Caribbean against challenges such as transnational crime; and how the regional economic commissions should be strengthened as instruments for sustainable development and reducing inequality.

Honduras

Mireya Agüero, Minister of Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Frames the guarantee of political dialogue as a process of democratisation within the UN's own organs, against a backdrop of proliferating armed conflict, rising geopolitical tension and growing polarisation between states. Asked the single most concrete question of the ministerial segment: what specific measures would a candidate advance to guarantee equitable geographic distribution in the appointment of senior and high-responsibility posts within the Secretariat. No candidate answered it with a target, a timeline or a procedure.

Paraguay

Víctor Verdún, Vice-Minister of Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Does not endorse a candidate and says Paraguay will support whoever is elected. Raises Haiti as a constant regional concern and asked what policies would guarantee successful implementation of Security Council resolution 2793, which converts the multinational security support mission into a new gang-suppression force. Also asked how cooperation between the United Nations and regional bodies such as Mercosur, the OAS and CELAC would be strengthened.

Suriname

Miriam MacIntosh, Permanent Secretary for Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Speaking for the Minister of Foreign Affairs, International Business and Cooperation. Calls the dialogue timely and appropriate after 80 years, and reaffirms the importance of a united regional voice for a more representative, inclusive, transparent, accountable and effective organisation. Asked two questions: how the candidates intend to ensure developing-country concerns and priorities, including the region's, are adequately reflected in the organisation's work; and how they would deploy resources and advocate for more of them given declining finances and earmarking. Closes by wishing all candidates success and naming the CARICOM-endorsed candidate.

Bolivia

Carlos Paz, Vice-Minister of Foreign Affairs

CELAC state

Argues the selection defines the political and moral leadership of the organisation rather than filling a senior administrative post. Wants independent leadership of integrity, deeply committed to the Charter, able to generate trust between states, facilitate dialogue and act with the moral authority to preserve peace and defend multilateralism. For the region and the Global South, says success will depend on building a more representative, democratic and equitable international system that corrects the asymmetries still limiting global governance. Presses preventive diplomacy, and argues peace cannot be reduced to the absence of armed confrontation — it requires legitimate institutions, cooperation and a commitment to international justice.
