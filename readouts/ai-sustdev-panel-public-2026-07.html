Executive summary

Pakistan and Tajikistan used an HLPF 2026 side event to put one proposition on the UN record: AI will either bridge the development divide or widen it, and the difference turns on the choices made now — above all, whether young people in developing countries are treated as innovators or left behind. The room was unusually unified. A Tajik minister, a Pakistani youth-program chairman, a UNDP advisor, a Google lead, a youth delegate, and floor interventions from Kazakhstan, China and Azerbaijan all converged on the same commitments.

The one voice that complicated the optimism: UNDP's Jennifer Lui warned that "our safeguards are not keeping pace with AI's growth," that three-quarters of the world's compute sits in a single country, and that jobless but digitally literate youth in the Global South "are being enlisted into cybercriminality." See stand-out interventions.

Four commitments the whole room shared — and named as the closing takeaways:

  • Bridge the AI divide — compute, finance, skills and data must reach developing countries. →
  • Turn AI opportunities into tangible development outcomes, equitably. →
  • Youth as shapers, not users — innovators and co-authors of AI governance. →
  • Human-centred, safe, trustworthy AI — "humanity must shape the future of AI." →

Underneath the consensus run three tensions of emphasis — finance vs. skills, sovereign capacity vs. Big-Tech partnership, and state optimism vs. UNDP's risk warning — differences in weight, not open disputes.

Governing frame — where this plugs in

The reference point four speakers kept returning to

UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance + Independent International Scientific Panel on AI

Held in Geneva the week before this event, the inaugural Global Dialogue and its Scientific Panel were cited by UNDP, Google, China and Pakistan's Permanent Representative alike as the frame for turning AI principles into practice. The Panel's preliminary warning — that safeguards are not keeping pace and the access gap risks widening inequality — was quoted approvingly across the room. Pakistan's PR framed the global approach as currently "fragmented," with this dialogue as the integrator.

  • No hard deadline emerged from this event; the actionable takeaway is which venue the agenda now runs through.
  • Parallel venue China announced the 2026 World AI Conference and high-level meeting on global AI governance, Shanghai, 17–20 July 2026 ("Intelligent Partners: Co-Creating the Future") — offered days before it opened, a competing/complementary track to the UN Geneva dialogue.
Where they agree — the shared frame
  • AI must bridge, not widen, the divide. The central risk everyone named: concentrated compute, finance, data and talent will deepen inequality without deliberate action. AI is not inherently inclusive. Its benefits will depend on the choices we make today… artificial intelligence may widen the very inequalities that sustainable development seeks to overcome. — Chairman Rana Mashood Ahmad Khan (Pakistan)
  • Youth are shapers, not just users. The organizing thesis of the whole event, echoed by China, Azerbaijan and the closing remarks. History will not remember the speed of AI that we adapted towards, but history will remember how we had the courage to include the young people while we were adapting AI. — Bisma Qamar (Pakistan, youth delegate)
  • AI must remain human-centred, safe and trustworthy. Transparency, human oversight, accountability. We say that artificial intelligence will shape our common future, but… it's the humanity that must shape the future of the AI. — Ambassador Asim Iftikhar Ahmed (Pakistan PR, closing)
  • AI can accelerate the SDGs — especially SDG 9. Innovation, resilient infrastructure, inclusive industrialization; named by Tajikistan, Pakistan, Azerbaijan and China. For developing countries, the promise of AI lies not in automation alone, but in its power to leapfrog traditional development barriers. — Deputy PR of China
  • International cooperation and capacity-building are the mechanism. Regional hubs, shared infrastructure, South-South and triangular cooperation — Tajikistan's IT Hub Dushanbe, Kazakhstan's Astana Hub, China's governance initiative, the UN dialogue. Developing countries should not merely adopt artificial intelligence, they should actively shape its future. — Permanent Representative of Kazakhstan

Tensions of emphasis — differences in weight, not open disputes
This room did not split into camps. But speakers weighted the problem differently, and those differences matter for where the agenda goes next. Each row shows the emphasis and the speaker's verbatim words — not opposing sides of a fight.

1 · Finance vs. skills — what actually bridges the divide?

Is the binding constraint money and compute that must move, or skills and capacity that countries can build?

The two emphases sit side by side without confrontation. The Pakistan chairman makes the sharper structural demand — investment and compute must move, and declarations are not enough. Most other speakers stress the more consensual, unilaterally actionable lever: skills, scholarships, academies, curricula.

Chairman Khan (Pakistan)Finance / investment

"The global AI divide cannot be bridged through declarations alone. It requires sustained investment, equitable access, and genuine international cooperation."

Hashim Saeed (Google)Skills / capacity

"Only 14% of workers have actually globally received formal and true AI training. So we have to bridge this gap through academia and enterprise."

Bisma Qamar (Pakistan)Platforms / funding

"Funding is almost the biggest challenge that youth face" — the barrier is platforms and access, not talent.

2 · Sovereign capacity vs. Big-Tech partnership

How do developing countries build sovereign AI while scaling through a single dominant vendor's tools?

Structural rather than voiced. Google frames the path as a transition to "localized, sovereign solutions" — while positioning its own products as the delivery mechanism. The Pakistan chairman names the guardrail directly. Neither treats the other as an opponent, but the sovereignty-vs-monopoly question is live in a room where a Big-Tech partner is presenting.

Hashim Saeed (Google)Scale via partnership

"A transition from passive consumption of global technologies to active, localized, sovereign solutions." (delivered via AI Seekho, Build with AI, Gemini in ministries)

Chairman Khan (Pakistan)Guardrail: no monopoly

"AI must remain human-centered and locally relevant. It must not become a tool of technological monopoly."

3 · State optimism vs. UNDP's risk warning

Is the story mostly national AI wins to celebrate, or risks that are outrunning the safeguards?

The floor interventions largely catalogue national successes. Against that optimistic register, UNDP's Jennifer Lui is the one voice pulling the other way — the compute gap, safeguards falling behind, and the darkest note of the day on jobless youth and cybercrime.

Kazakhstan / China / AzerbaijanShowcase optimism

China: "Our intelligent manufacturing systems have boosted efficiency by over 30% while cutting defects by half… digital public service platforms now reach more than 90% of rural populations."

Jennifer Lui (UNDP)Safeguards lagging

"Our safeguards are not keeping pace with AI's growth. And the gap in access — in compute, in skills, in infrastructure — risks exacerbating the inequalities into the next generation."

Stand-out interventions — the ones worth reading twice

The compute-concentration + safeguards warning — Jennifer Lui, UNDP

The event's most substantive departure from the celebratory tone. Concrete UNDP evidence: ~25 AI landscape assessments worldwide; RapidAssist post-disaster AI delivering 72-hour assessments in 20+ countries (350,000 people reached in Afghanistan after the 2025 earthquake; redeployed in Venezuela); the AI Hub for Sustainable Development (with Italy) supporting 18 African countries on green compute. Her governance thesis is the intellectual high point: AI governance is "being decided in many smaller choices… micro-governance choices you're making, whether or not you call it that."

The conflict-state edge case — The Aung Hwan, Myanmar

The one voice from a conflict-affected / least-developed context. Since the 2021 coup, Myanmar's digital development "deteriorated." Presses "leave no one behind" to its hardest case — vulnerable youth in conflict zones — and, tellingly, drew no substantive answer in the panel's closing round.

The procurement-access admission — Qasim (Neural Lab) → Jennifer Lui

Asked how startups can actually contract with UNDP and the multilateral space, Lui was unusually candid: even UNDP has not codified its AI procurement practices, they are "not visible," and that opacity is itself a barrier — "I will also admit that even our own organization here is still kind of working through what those procurement practices need to be."

China's parallel-venue pitch — Deputy PR of China

Beyond the national showcase, China used the floor to announce the 2026 World AI Conference and high-level meeting on global AI governance (Shanghai, 17–20 July 2026, "Intelligent Partners: Co-Creating the Future") — a parallel governance track offered days before it opened, alongside the UN's Geneva dialogue.

The four priorities, named on the record. Pakistan's PR closed by distilling the discussion into four takeaways — matching the four the Pakistan chairman set out in his keynote, giving the event a single throughline:
