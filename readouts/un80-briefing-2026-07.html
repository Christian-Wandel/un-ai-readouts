Executive Summary

On 29 July 2026 the General Assembly met informally for the seventh monthly briefing on the UN80 Initiative, the Secretary-General's reform of the United Nations. Three separate reform packages were presented in a single sitting, and 34 delegations responded — each with one three-minute turn covering all three. This readout keeps the three analyses separate, because each will have its own future meetings, and then reads them together, because they happened in one room.

This was a first airing, not a negotiation. Packages 18 and 21 had never been presented in this setting before, and nothing in any of the three has been negotiated. Wandel said plainly that he might not be able to answer concrete implementation questions. That sets what is worth reading for here: where delegations converge, which ideas already have backing, and who to approach to carry each one forward. Points that remain open are recorded as open, not as positions taken.

The three packages, in plain terms:

  • Work package 18 — funding. How the UN is paid for. Most of its money is “earmarked” by donors for named projects rather than given as flexible funding — core and pooled money the UN can allocate against agreed priorities. The proposal treats core, pooled and earmarked money as one portfolio and asks agencies to decline earmarked funding that costs more to administer than it is worth.
  • Work package 21 — results. Whether anyone can draw a line from a mandate the membership gives the UN, to the money spent on it, to what actually happened. The proposal is a new system-wide mechanism plus a regular dialogue between UN leadership and member states.
  • Work package 22 — peace. Consolidating the UN's prevention and peacebuilding tools. Unlike the other two, this one is already built: the Peacebuilding and Peace Support Office opened in January 2026, and a map of deployable expertise is due in September.

Where the room converged

One question runs through all three packages: how much new machinery the membership is ready to authorise, and who steers it. Across the three, two things were widely shared — delegations did not argue for keeping things as they are, and they looked for assurances before backing a new instrument. Each package found support at a different depth.

  • Package 18 found the session's clearest cross-bloc agreement. Recommendation 5 — discipline in accepting earmarked funding — was backed by Ethiopiabrief, United Kingdom, Pakistan and Switzerland — an African least-developed country, a P5 donor, a G77 heavyweight and a European contributor on the same line. Protecting core funding and settling unpaid contributions drew equally broad agreement.
  • Package 21 was welcomed in principle, with the design still open. European Union, France and Romania set out the most developed reading of a new mechanism. A larger group welcomed the direction while asking for detail on scope, cost and governance first. The Secretariat placed it at the same stage, describing package 21 as “only generated as a concept”. One request united nearly the whole room: no new reporting burden.
  • Package 22 drew broad warmth and few requests. Because the office already exists, most delegations had nothing to authorise, and the discussion turned to how it will work in practice. Mexico, Germany, European Union and CANZ (Australia) asked for additions.

Support is package-specific

Each package drew its own coalition, and they are not the same coalition. A delegation engaged on one package is not automatically a supporter on the next, so the practical read is per package: for any one of the three, the table below shows who spoke for it and how strongly. Individual delegations also read differently from their group statement — Senegal and Ethiopiabrief both reach the top band on funding, above the G77 + China (Uruguay) statement they sit under. Worth approaching delegations individually rather than through their group.

What the Secretariat Proposed

Three Secretariat officials briefed. Guy Ryder (USG Policy), Under-Secretary-General for Policy, gave the overall update; Elizabeth Spehar (ASG Peacebuilding), Assistant Secretary-General for Peacebuilding and Peace Support, presented package 22; and Jens Wandel (Special Adviser on Reforms), Special Adviser to the Secretary-General on Reforms, presented packages 18 and 21 together.

The question underneath packages 18 and 21

Wandel framed both packages around a single problem: whether the UN can show what it did with what it was given. He put it as a question the system cannot currently answer, and paired the two packages as managing for results and financing for results (full quote below).

What the Secretariat reported as already done

  • ECOSOC adopted the QCPR resolution by consensus, which Ryder said reaffirms reconfiguring country teams, expanding shared back-office services and recalibrating the resident coordinator system.
  • A unified services roadmap and shared-services feasibility study were published on 1 July, with claimed savings of 20 to 80% for key back-office services.
  • A GA resolution on strengthening ECOSOC asks it to apply the outcomes of the mandate implementation review in its own decision-making.
  • The Peacebuilding and Peace Support Office has been operating since January 2026.
  • The Secretary-General met 500 civil society representatives on 9 July; Ryder reported their message as renewal that is “shaped with people and not only for them”.

What was put to member states as new

  • Package 18: ten recommendations. The ones that drew comment were repositioning the Joint SDG Fund as a Secretary-General's Fund for Development (rec 3), a quality-of-earmarking framework with differentiated cost recovery (recs 4–6), demonstrating what core funding enables (rec 7), an annual funding compact review (rec 9), and stronger country-level funding dialogues led by resident coordinators (rec 10).
  • Package 21: a system-wide management-for-results mechanism, described as “light touch”, built on common standards for how results are articulated and linked to resources — explicitly not a common taxonomy across entities. To be developed progressively by the end of 2027.
  • Package 22: the mapping under action 55 of surge capacity across DPO, DPPA, the Office of Disarmament Affairs and the UN Office of Counter-Terrorism, with results due September 2026.

Status matters here. Wandel stated plainly that this was the first time packages 18 and 21 had been presented in this setting, and that he might not be able to answer concrete implementation questions. Nothing in either package has been negotiated.

Where to look for support on each package

  • Package 18 — funding. The strongest advocacy came from Switzerland, United Kingdom, CANZ (Australia), European Union, Senegal and Ethiopiabrief. Donors and recipients arrive from different directions — Switzerland and the United Kingdom accept constraints on their own giving, Senegal and Ethiopia want recipient priorities protected — and both routes lead to recommendation 5.
  • Package 21 — results. European Union, France and Romania carry the case here, with United Kingdom, CANZ (Australia), Republic of Korea, Armenia and Senegal engaged on design. France offers the one concrete institutional hook on the table — tying the performance dialogue to the annual programme-budget presentation.
  • Package 22 — peace. Mexico, Germany, European Union, CANZ (Australia), Croatia, Angola and Egypt engaged most actively. Mexico is the only delegation asking for both rapid-deployment mechanisms and regular-budget financing for prevention; Germany is the largest contributor to the Peacebuilding Fund.
  • Donor positions are package-specific. Switzerland and United Kingdom put their weight on funding, Germany on peacebuilding, France on results. Worth approaching each on the package they engaged with rather than treating them as one bloc.
  • Shared ground between delegations that differ elsewhere. Cuba and Croatia use closely similar language on not securitising peace and draw different institutional conclusions — Cuba to keep the pillars apart, Croatia to bring them together. Japan and Russian Federation both question central pooling, Japan on donor visibility and budget autonomy, Russia on the administrative-cost premise. Common language is a route into a conversation even where the conclusions differ.
  • Four delegations used their whole turn on one package: Ethiopiabrief on funding; Croatia, Slovenia and Egypt on peacebuilding. With three minutes and three packages, that choice indicates where their priority sits.
Work package 18

Review of Funding Mechanisms

How the UN is paid for: core, pooled and earmarked money treated as one portfolio.

Areas of Consensus — Work Package 18

Nobody defended the status quo. The agreement is real, and it crosses the usual blocs.

  • The diagnosis is accepted. Senegal called the growing weight of earmarked funding against squeezed core funding a source of major concern, and no delegation disputed it.
  • Core funding must be protected and better justified. G77 + China (Uruguay) called it indispensable; CANZ (Australia) and United Kingdom both singled out recommendation 7 as the way to rebuild donor confidence. The same recommendation, from opposite motives — recipients want the floor protected, donors want the case made.
  • Recommendation 5 is the closest thing to a cross-bloc win in the whole session. Ethiopiabrief, United Kingdom, Pakistan and Switzerland all backed discipline on accepting earmarked funding (quotes below).
  • Unpaid contributions are raised across the room. European Union and CANZ (Australia) call for payment in full and on time; Iran and Cuba make the same call, Iran adding that contributions should come without conditions and without actions impeding other states from paying.
  • Country-level dialogue should use the cooperation framework, not add a layer. Senegal and Armenia both want existing machinery used.
Open Questions — Work Package 18
1. Who controls the money — the Joint SDG Fund

An open question in the package, and one that would benefit from further discussion.

For elevating it: European Union wants it strengthened as the principal catalytic instrument. Zimbabwe welcomes the repositioning. Senegal says it deserves full attention.

Questions still open: Russian Federation argues the change does not address the fund's accountability gap, and states that allocation being decided by internal organs the Secretariat itself selects is unacceptable. Maldives asks the procedural question — does it need member-state approval? Pakistan asks what changes beyond the name. China raises possible duplication with CERF and the Peacebuilding Fund. Namibia raises an operational point others did not: that centralising may reduce impact on the ground.

Where the Secretariat left it. Wandel set out the rationale — coherence, and balance against CERF and the Peacebuilding Fund — and took note of the views expressed. As a first presentation, the design remains open.

2. Does earmarking discipline constrain donors or protect recipients?

Both camps back recommendation 5 and mean different things by it.

The recipient reading: Ethiopiabrief argues earmarked money is supply-driven and may not match what member states collectively agreed. Senegal raises donor conditioning and distortions that can sideline recipient priorities. Iran notes earmarking risks distorting priorities and compromising impartial mandate implementation.

The donor reading: Japan wants national budgetary systems and policy objectives respected. Republic of Korea asks four detailed questions about how quality assessment would affect donor choice and whether new dialogues create consultation obligations. Mexico, a top-20 contributor, wants flexibility without losing the ability to direct its own money.

The donors who accept the constraint are United Kingdom and Switzerland — which is why they top this package's plot.

The unlikely pair: Russian Federation and Japan, politically opposite, both resist central pooling — Russia challenging the claim that pooled funding is cheaper to administer, Japan guarding donor visibility and budget autonomy.

3. Will reform cost developing countries their programmes?

G77 + China (Uruguay) sets the boundary: the review must not come at developing countries' expense. Rwanda asks the practical version — under financial stress, how can the UN be selective about earmarked money without affecting existing country programmes? It pairs the question with a commitment: “you can count on our support going forward”. Maldives raises the small-state variant. Wandel answered both in his closing remarks: quality is about how earmarked funding is structured rather than its size, and the aim is a dialogue with governments in which the transaction costs of accepting less aligned funding are visible to both sides. Cuba questions the premise that the crisis is a spending problem. Angola wants equitable allocation to Africa, LDCs and conflict-affected states. Suriname wants equitable access preserved for all programme countries.

4. Who pays, and does reform let anyone off the hook?

China states it most directly: traditional donors keep primary responsibility. Angola asks that voluntary money complement rather than replace assessed contributions. Against this, the donor emphasis is value for money as the precondition for volume — CANZ (Australia) on demonstrating value, Romania on contributors needing to see dividends. Germany puts the obligation back on member states while asking the system to earn it through accountability and contributor visibility.

Outliers

  • France announced the only new money in the session: $5 million to the Horizon Palestine Fund, described as a pilot.
  • El Salvador raised an institutional question that remains open: how do the recommendations interact with the business models of UNDP and UNICEF?
  • Pakistan reserved on the proposed common portfolio framework spanning humanitarian, development and peacebuilding funds.
  • Switzerland proposed importing lessons from the humanitarian pillar.
  • Indonesia introduced a metric nobody else used — the programme expense ratio, to keep funds from being consumed by overhead.

Work package 21

System-Wide Management for Results

Whether anyone can trace a mandate to the money spent on it and the result achieved.

Areas of Consensus — Work Package 21

The diagnosis was accepted without dissent. What follows it was not.

  • The mandate–resource–result line should exist. Rwanda told Ryder directly that this link is extremely important.
  • Three guiding principles were endorsed almost universally — and read back as conditions. Malaysia stated them most completely: build on existing frameworks, avoid duplication and added reporting burdens, respect entity governance — and asked that these principles stay central as the mechanism develops.
  • “No new reporting burden” is the most widely shared request of the session. G77 + China (Uruguay), Pacific Islands Forum (Solomon Is.), CANZ (Australia), Zimbabwe, Cuba, Suriname, Malaysia, Pakistan, Iran, Switzerland and Maldives all made it.
  • Focus on a limited number of outcomes. CANZ (Australia) on a limited set of agreed system-wide outcomes; United Kingdom on prioritising outcomes and impact over activities.
  • Better data is a precondition for mandate review. European Union argues member states cannot review mandates without evidence-based data from the Secretariat.
Open Questions — Work Package 21
1. Is this a management tool or a governance mechanism?

The open question in the package: who the mechanism ultimately serves.

A leadership instrument: France offers the most operationally ambitious reading, wanting the performance dialogue tied to the annual programme-budget presentation and the framework applied to a country or region. Romania frames it as a renewed management performance paradigm. Armenia welcomes leadership-level dialogue. European Union fully supports harmonisation and wants the design as concrete as possible.

An accountability instrument answerable to members: Russian Federation asks whether a new structure is needed, arguing the underlying issue is internal coordination rather than missing mechanisms. G77 + China (Uruguay) and Iran both ask that the Secretariat play a neutral role. Malaysia wants clarity on the respective roles of Secretariat and member states. United Kingdom puts the purpose question directly; it remains open.

The Secretariat validated the control-side reading. Wandel confirmed the members' reading of the intent was correct — not a technocratic exercise, not a new layer — and named CANZ (Australia), Malaysia, Armenia and Senegal as having read it right. Those four sit in different camps on other questions.

2. Will results measurement become a budget-cutting instrument?

Raised by a few delegations, and a concern worth addressing in the design. Cuba states it directly, cautioning against automatic justification of cuts and against efficiency becoming an end in itself. Zimbabwe asks how results-based management links to allocation in a way that is fair to structurally vulnerable countries. Angola puts it more broadly: reform should strengthen delivery rather than drive downsizing.

Romania makes the funding connection explicit and positive, and France says better measurement could guide future funding. Both readings describe the same mechanism — one sees results guiding money, the other wants safeguards on that same link. Clarifying it would likely bring the two closer.

3. Whose yardstick, and does it fit small states?

Cuba wants intergovernmentally agreed indicators. Indonesia wants the mechanism grounded in country realities, national statistics and cooperation-framework outcomes. Maldives raises the measurement-validity problem for small island states, where scale alone cannot demonstrate results. Timor-Leste notes it matters most for countries with limited administrative capacity. Against this, European Union suggests importing methodologies from other international organisations.

Wandel's answer — standards for how results are articulated and linked to resources, not a common taxonomy — is aimed here. Whether it satisfies the small-state objection is untested.

4. Cost and timeline — the detail delegations asked for

European Union asks for the budgetary implications of the performance dialogues; United Kingdom asks for implementation costs. For a mechanism described as light touch, the cost is not yet stated — supplying it would answer several delegations at once. Republic of Korea asks about the end-2027 timeline and requests the concept and implementation plan early. Namibia considers the timeline realistic.

Outliers

  • El Salvador asks that the work proceed without new instruments.
  • France proposed the one concrete institutional hook on the table; no other delegation took it up, so it is still available to build on.
  • Maldives asked the only cross-package sequencing question and requested a consolidated implementation map. Directly relevant to anyone leading both files.
  • Brazilbrief asked when member states will actually get the data they need for mandate review, and received the session's most specific commitment: September.
  • Rwanda asked for the volume of UN80 material to be made easier to absorb — a genuine signal about how much delegations are being asked to process.

Work package 22

Prevention, Peacebuilding and Peace Support

Consolidating the UN's prevention and peacebuilding tools. This one is already built.

Areas of Consensus — Work Package 22

Broad agreement on the direction of this package, with the detail still to be worked through.

  • Prevention is cheaper than response. Stated repeatedly, never contested. Suriname and Timor-Leste both make the case, Timor-Leste from direct experience.
  • Reducing fragmentation is the right objective. United Kingdom and China agree on this much.
  • National ownership is non-negotiable. Cuba says the UN should support rather than replace state-set priorities; China that the office should respect and leverage country leadership.
  • Peace and development advisers are valued and under-resourced. Germany hopes they increase in number; European Union stresses predictable resourcing; Pacific Islands Forum (Solomon Is.) and CANZ (Australia) back regional advisers hosted in regional commissions.
  • Mission transitions are a genuine gap. Japan highlights financial and security gaps during drawdown; Germany calls for flexible pass-through funding.
Open Questions — Work Package 22
1. Is prevention development work or security work?

The open question in the package, and the readings follow group lines fairly closely.

Guard development from securitisation: Cuba puts it most fully, cautioning against a securitized approach and arguing prevention means addressing poverty, inequality and underdevelopment. G77 + China (Uruguay) asks that development activities stay focused on poverty eradication and that the resident coordinator system's primary purpose remain development. China places development first explicitly.

Integrate across pillars: CANZ (Australia) argues the opposite direction — prevention is system-wide, so the UN should think beyond the traditional peace and security architecture. Suriname ties peacebuilding to climate resilience and the 2030 Agenda.

Shared language worth noting: Cuba and Croatia both caution against securitisation in near-identical language and draw different institutional conclusions — Cuba to keep the pillars apart so development is not absorbed, Croatia to bring them together so prevention is not left to security actors alone. Same diagnosis, different prescription, and a starting point either could build from.

On how the discussions relate: G77 + China (Uruguay) states that this work and the regional reset and expertise-on-demand discussions are two distinct conversations. Spehar indicated an intent to connect them, and Croatia and Indonesia ask how they connect — so the relationship between the two tracks is worth settling early.

2. Does consolidation strengthen capacity or dilute it?

Supportive: Egypt calls the restructuring a step in the right direction. Armenia welcomes the office as a central platform while naming the institutional challenges. France welcomes it for technical expertise and operational delivery.

Supportive with conditions: Russian Federation asks that the merger not reduce the quality and volume of security sector reform and DDR work — support conditional on no capacity loss. Angola asks that consolidation preserve dedicated expertise, accountability and distinct mandates.

A question from the review's own facilitator: Slovenia, which facilitated the peacebuilding architecture review, asks whether the merged structure is fit for purpose and able to support an ambitious incoming Secretary-General, and what its relationship to the Mediation Support Unit is.

The governance gap: Egypt identifies it precisely — the office is described as a centre of excellence, a bridge and a hinge, but decision-making and coordination with other entities remain unclear. It wants the office empowered, with clear governance arrangements and success indicators.

3. Is the mapping a service to the field or a headquarters exercise?

The most practical of the three questions, and the one with a test attached. United Kingdom sets it: success depends on whether resident coordinators and country teams experience a clearer practical offer rather than a headquarters inventory. Indonesia asks how surge capacity avoids duplicating regional frameworks and how the mapping becomes usable for timely deployment. Pacific Islands Forum (Solomon Is.) asks for something the mapping does not currently promise — expertise concentrated at subregional level. El Salvador asks what results are expected from it.

Spehar accepted the framing, describing the output as a menu of options — what exists, where, how financed, how accessed — and was unambiguous that the office is a facilitator delivering through country teams with the resident coordinator's steer central.

4. Regional organisations — partner or subcontractor?

Egypt asks how the comparative advantages of regional organisations such as the African Union are reflected operationally under subsidiarity. Angola asks for process rights, naming the African Union, ICGLR and SADC, and consultation before changes to regional political offices. Zimbabwe supports deploying skills on demand for regional initiatives. Pacific Islands Forum (Solomon Is.) makes the subregional case for the Pacific.

Still to be addressed. Spehar's reply covered resident coordinators and system partners; the regional-organisation and subsidiarity dimension is open for the next round.

Outliers

  • Slovenia connected the discussion to the succession, noting that all six candidates to be the next Secretary-General put peace and security first — which reframes the office's adequacy as a question about readiness for the next administration.
  • Mexico proposed three things nobody else did: political support for appointing experienced envoys, predictable regular-budget financing for good offices and mediation, and light rapid-deployment mechanisms for ceasefires where no mission exists.
  • Zimbabwe made the session's only reference to women, peace and security.
  • Rwanda asked what “peace finance” actually means, prompting Spehar's fullest answer on innovative financing.
  • Angola made the cross-package link explicit: prevention cannot be prioritised institutionally while remaining underfunded operationally.

What Spehar answered

The most substantive reply of the three briefers. On regional advisers, the impediment is financial; current capacity is 27 international advisers, 24 national officers, 2 UN Volunteers and 9 secondees. On national prevention strategies, she gave a worked example: in Papua New Guinea the adviser supported the strategy, the Peacebuilding Fund financed agencies, and the World Bank then approved its first prevention and resilience allocation outside Africa. On peace finance, current mechanisms are insufficient and the aim is an ecosystem drawing in international financial institutions and private investment. On mediation, the Peacebuilding Fund can finance long-term local and civil-society capacity that DPPA's mediation support does not — currently in Bougainville, Papua New Guinea.

The timing that frames all of it

The Secretary-General's mandate ends this year. Ryder said he intends to stay engaged “right up until the last day”, and Slovenia noted that all six candidates to succeed him put peace and security first. A funding architecture and a results mechanism agreed under one Secretary-General would be implemented by the next.
